What the sketch became

Twelve decisions, each for a reason.

  1. The step is crisp.

    M1's edge ends about 2 mm before the screen's glass begins, so there's no room for a soft curve. The glass deck runs straight into the step's face: one block standing on a dark pane.

  2. The lower level's top is dark glass.

    A flush pane of grey-smoke acrylic instead of an opening. The octopus glows up out of the dark, someone across the table sees all of it, and it wipes clean. It's the move Dieter Rams and Hans Gugelot made on the Braun SK4 in 1956: a clear lid over the works. The pane rests on a ledge inside the walls, which has a 45° chamfer under it so it prints without support.

  3. The screen is the recording light.

    No LED is added (15 Oct). The screen is always on, dim, and while recording it shows REC and the time under the glass. The boards' own lights stay as they are: M1's charge light should show through the USB-C opening (not checked), and EB's green power light sits under the lid.

  4. The controls go where the boards put them.

    Start/stop (D1) is on the front, the side people face. The battery switch is on the back. Reset and USB-C share M1's end. The control you use daily faces you; the rarely used ones face away.

  5. Start/stop is a flexing tongue.

    D1 sits 0.6 mm behind the wall, too close for a loose plunger with a collar to hold it in. Instead a tongue cut into the wall presses D1 with a nub on its back: a plunger with no loose part. It's hinged at one end, so it bends along the print layers, not across them, where printed parts crack. The hinge is thinned to 0.7 mm, which makes the press about 1 N on top of D1's own spring (a beam estimate; the test ring below checks it). A raised dot over the nub shows where to press, and the firmware's 2-second hold still guards against accidental starts.

  6. Reset is a pinhole.

    A paper clip reaches the button; a finger can't. A reset mid-recording loses up to two seconds of audio, so it shouldn't be easy to press.

  7. The battery switch gets a slot and a thumb scallop.

    The lever stays inside the wall line, so nothing snags, and the scallop gives your thumb room to push it. With the LiPo, it's a real off.

  8. The microphone gets its own port.

    One 1.6 mm hole straight above the mic. The mic sits on the Sense board, below the camera's height, so a short duct reaches down to it, and a foam ring seals the duct to the mic. Without the seal, sound fills the box first and comes out boomy. A faint ring around the hole shows people where it listens.

  9. The camera stays, behind a cap.

    A 7 mm port sits over its lens, for later. Until then a cap fills it flush, with a rim inside so it can't be pushed in. Nobody at the table sees a lens, and the port is ready when the camera is wanted. As M1 stands, the lens looks straight up.

  10. Air rises through the tall end.

    Cool air comes in through the far end and the floor; warm air leaves through six slots high on the back of the tall end, the way a chimney draws. The warm parts are the ESP32 and the battery charger, which runs hottest while charging. The battery sits lowest, in the coolest air: a LiPo shouldn't be charged above about 45 °C.

  11. Nothing on top but the glass, the mic and the cap.

    Four screws hold EB to the bottom plate and four more hold the plate to the case from underneath. Four stick-on feet stop it sliding and keep table knocks away from the mic. The Wi-Fi antenna sticks inside the tall end's front wall, high up, where about 17 mm of air separates it from M1 and nothing metal is near.

  12. PETG, not PLA.

    PLA starts to soften around 60 °C, PETG around 80 °C. That margin matters for a closed box that charges a battery through a hot summer.

Useful

Every opening has a job: mic, start/stop, reset, switch, USB-C with the microSD card's slot over it, air, and the camera's port for later. There are no others, and the screen reads from across the table.

Understandable

One control faces people, and it's the one you use. The others sit at the back and the end.

Unobtrusive

One colour and dark glass, no logo, the vents at the back and underneath. The screen is dim until something changes.

Honest

The mic port is visible, the screen always shows when it's recording, and the camera is capped until it's used.

Thorough

Glass that sits flush and fits either way round, a shadow gap round the start/stop tongue, a cap you can't push in, hidden screws, soft feet.

As little design as possible

The step is there because M1 stands on EB. The shape is the stack plus clearance.

Six of Dieter Rams' ten principles for good design, the ones that decided something here.

Before the real print

Print the cheap tests first.

  1. The bottom plate (about an hour). Checks the four holes and the battery bay.

  2. A 12 mm ring of the case at button height: in the slicer, cut the case at 7 and 19 mm and print the middle. Checks how start/stop feels, and that the reset pinhole and the switch slot line up, before the long print.

  3. The case, standing upright, with tree supports inside; the open bottom lets them out. Print the camera cap with it: it takes minutes.

  4. The glass: @@DECK@@ from 2 mm grey-smoke acrylic, corners R@@DECK_R@@. Score and snap it, then file the corners, or send out/deck.svg to a laser-cutting shop. Hold a light and a dark sample over the running screen first: the dim always-on screen has to show through. Fix it with clear silicone or thin clear tape on the ledge, not superglue, which fogs acrylic white.

  5. Fit the parts inside. The cap goes into its port from inside before EB goes in. Stick a foam ring, about 4 mm across with a 1.5 mm hole, on the end of the mic's duct. EB's corner holes go over the four pins on the plate's standoffs: they keep the buttons' plungers off the wall. The battery is a 603040 flat LiPo (6 × 30 × 40 mm) with a JST-PH 2.0 plug: check its polarity with a multimeter first.

  6. Then measure three things. The mic: record the same 30 seconds at 1 m with and without the case, and compare levels with bench analyse. Heat: the ESP32-S3 has a temperature sensor, and the firmware could report it in /status, a small change for rec-12. The screen: through the glass it looks dimmer; check the dim always-on screen still reads from across the table.

Decided on 6 Oct

Your answers, and what's left.

  1. The top: dark glass

    Someone across the table sees the whole screen, against @@SEEN_W@@ of it through an opening, and there's no well to collect crumbs. It costs one small piece of acrylic and some cutting.

  2. The recording light: the screen (changed 15 Oct)

    No LED is added. The boards' own lights can't show recording (M1's user LED is wired to the SD card's chip-select, and the charge and power lights show only power), so the screen does: REC and the time, always on, under the glass.

  3. The battery: a flat LiPo

    A 603040 cell (6 × 30 × 40 mm) with a JST-PH 2.0 plug, so the switch is a real off. The bay is drawn for it. On a cell this small, expect hours rather than a full day; the recorder's current draw hasn't been measured. At home it runs on USB.

  4. The camera: kept, with a capped port

    For an enhancement later. As M1 stands, the lens looks straight up at the ceiling. If the enhancement needs to see people at the table, the camera would have to face sideways, which means a different mount for it; its ribbon looks short in Seeed's photo.

  5. The pin headers: left as they are

    With the glass they cost only 2.5 mm of height, and cutting them can't be undone.

  6. Still needed: the three photos

    Every position marked Check above comes from Seeed's photos and drawings. The photos replace them, and then the case can be printed.
